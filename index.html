Abstract

Reliability in Event-Driven Design

How Correctness Boundaries Shape Process-Handling Choices and Recovery Behaviour

Background

Reliability in event-driven processes is difficult to judge when what a process must protect is left implicit. Event flows may preserve, delay, replay, duplicate, or omit work, but these behaviours do not carry a fixed process-level meaning. Their significance depends on what must remain true for a case to count as acceptable. Without that prior account of correctness, the value of a handling choice becomes harder to assess.

Objectives

This thesis develops a boundary-first framework for reasoning about reliability in event-driven processes. The aim is to clarify how explicit correctness boundaries shape process-handling choices in systems where asynchronous event processing performs correctness-relevant work.

Methods

The thesis follows a design science research approach. In design-science terms, the framework is the central artifact, while the guide developed later gives that framework a more concrete form. The framework is examined through a controlled empirical suite. The suite isolates three working boundary classes: deadline-constrained, state-oriented, and required-effect. These are examined across three process-handling models: transient/immediate, retained/immediate, and retained/deferred.

Results

Within the controlled scenarios examined here, the results show that the value of replay, retention, immediate handling, and deferred settlement depends on the correctness boundary being protected. For a deadline-constrained condition, recovery matters only while unsettled work can still be repaid before expiry. For a state-oriented condition, replay matters when missed state remains relevant, while later state may also overtake earlier misses. For a required-effect condition, replay can recover downstream handling gaps after emission, but not source omission, and deferred settlement reduces one kind of immediate pressure by moving part of the burden into later reconciliation.

Conclusions

The thesis contributes a process-level framework for comparing handling choices under explicit correctness requirements. Its value lies in making the reasoning inspectable: what condition or set of conditions is protected, how those conditions could fail, which handling model is chosen, and what trade-off follows. The guide gives this reasoning a more concrete form, while the contribution remains the framework itself and the disciplined comparison it makes possible.
